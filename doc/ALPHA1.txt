================================================================================
Hol ALPHA1 — Language and Runtime Specification
================================================================================

Status
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This document specifies the Hol ALPHA1 dialect shipped by `ppap` under the
dispatcher command `ALPHA1`. ALPHA1 is the minimal member of the Hol family: a
simply typed, higher-order logic-programming language in the lambda-Prolog
tradition with hereditary Harrop goals, higher-order pattern unification under
a scope-level discipline, Prolog-style cut, and natural-number arithmetic, and
nothing beyond that. The dialect is kept deliberately small: features of the
later dialects are not ported into it, only its defects are fixed, and this
document describes the implementation in `src/Hol/ALPHA1/` as it behaves.

Chapters 1 through 5 are normative for ALPHA1. Chapter 6 lists the observable
differences from Hol BETA (`doc/HolBETA.txt`) and Chapter 7 maps the
specification onto the Haskell modules; both are informative. The words MUST,
MUST NOT, and MAY have their usual specification meanings. The regression
suite named in Chapter 7 pins the behaviors that are intended.


================================================================================
Chapter 0. Vocabulary and Conventions
================================================================================

0.1 Programs, clauses, and goals
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A *program* is the contents of one `.hol` file: a sequence of declarations. A
*clause* (also *fact*) is a declaration that is a term of type `o`. A *query*
is a goal typed at the REPL prompt. A *goal* is a term of type `o` that the
runtime proves; a *hypothesis* is a clause installed locally by `=>` for the
duration of one goal. ALPHA1 runs exactly one program at a time: the built-in
declarations plus at most one loaded file. There are no modules and no
imports.

0.2 Constants and variables
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

An identifier bound by `\` is a lambda-bound variable, whatever its spelling.
Otherwise an identifier beginning with a lower-case letter names a *constant*,
which is a type constructor in a type and a declared data constructor or
predicate in a term, and an identifier beginning with an upper-case letter or
`_` is a *variable*: free in a clause, it is implicitly universally quantified
over the clause and is freshened on every use of the clause, and free in a
query, it is a *logic variable* whose binding the answer reports. A term is
*rigid* when its head is a constant or a bound variable and *flexible* when
its head is a logic variable.

0.3 Levels
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every logic variable and every constant introduced by `pi` carries a *level*,
a natural number. A query starts at level 0; `pi X\ G` introduces its constant
at the next deeper level and proves `G` there. Levels implement the scoping of
universal quantification: a logic variable can never be instantiated with a
constant introduced deeper than the variable itself (Section 4.2).

0.4 Order convention
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

"Source order" is top to bottom within a file and left to right within a
term. Proof search is depth-first and left-to-right, and "first" means visited
first by that search. Sequences in this document preserve source order.


================================================================================
Chapter 1. Lexical Structure
================================================================================

1.1 Characters and whitespace
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Outside comments and literals, source text consists of printable ASCII
characters (U+0020 through U+007E) and the four whitespace characters space,
horizontal tab, carriage return, and line feed. Whitespace separates tokens
and has no other meaning. Positions in diagnostics are 1-based (row, column)
pairs.

Lexing uses maximal munch: at each position the longest match wins, and among
rules matching the same text the rule listed first in Section 1.3 wins. Hence
a reserved word is reserved only when it is not a proper prefix of a longer
identifier: `s` is the successor constructor, `sx` is an identifier, and `is`
followed by a letter is part of an identifier.

1.2 Comments
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`%` begins a comment that extends to the end of the line. `(*` begins a block
comment that extends to the first following `*)`; block comments do not nest.
Comments may contain any character. A `(*` without a closing `*)` is not a
comment: its characters are tokenized as `(` and `*`, which normally surfaces
as a parse error.

1.3 Tokens
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The tokens, in the order of the lexer's rule table, are:

  punctuation   .  ->  =>  (  )  [  ]  ?-  ,  :-  =  is  =:=  =\=  <  =<  >
                >=  +  -  *  /  //  div  mod  rem  s  pi  sigma  ;  !  true
                fail  debug  \  ::  kind  type
  small-id      [a-z] [A-Za-z0-9_]*
  large-id      [A-Z_] [A-Za-z0-9_]*
  nat-lit       [0-9]+
  str-lit       '"' str-char* '"'
  chr-lit       '\'' chr-char '\''

The reserved words are `kind`, `type`, `pi`, `sigma`, `s`, `true`, `fail`,
`debug`, `is`, `div`, `mod`, and `rem`. A reserved word is never a `small-id`.
The spelling `_` alone is a `large-id` with the wildcard meaning of Section
3.4; a `large-id` such as `_X` is an ordinary variable name.

`str-char` is a printable ASCII character other than `\` and `"`, or an
escape; `chr-char` is a printable ASCII character other than `\` and `'`, or
an escape. The escapes are `\n`, `\t`, `\\`, `\"`, and `\'`. A natural literal
is a non-negative decimal integer of unbounded magnitude.

1.4 Lexical errors
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When no rule matches at some position, analysis stops with

  *** lexing error at { row = R, col = C }.

where (R, C) is the position of the offending character. Non-ASCII text
outside comments is such an error.


================================================================================
Chapter 2. Grammar
================================================================================

2.1 Programs and queries
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  program     ::= declaration*
  declaration ::= 'kind' small-id kind '.'
                | 'type' small-id type '.'
                | term '.'
  query       ::= '?-' term '.'

A file is read as a `program` and a REPL line as a `query`. The parser accepts
either form from the same start symbol, so a declaration typed at the prompt
is rejected with "it is not a query" and a file whose text is a query with
"it is not a program". An empty file is a valid, empty program.

2.2 Kinds and types
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  kind    ::= kind1 '->' kind
            | kind1
  kind1   ::= 'type'
            | '(' kind ')'

  type    ::= type1 '->' type
            | type1
  type1   ::= type1 type2
            | type2
  type2   ::= large-id
            | small-id
            | '(' type ')'

`->` is right-associative in kinds and in types. Type application is
left-associative and binds tighter than `->`. In a type, a `large-id` is a
type variable and a `small-id` is a type constructor.

2.3 Terms
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  term            ::= large-id '\' term
                    | small-id '\' term
                    | term1 ':-' term
                    | term1
  term1           ::= term1 ';' term2
                    | term2
  term2           ::= term3 '=>' term2
                    | term3
  term3           ::= term3 ',' term4
                    | term4
  term4           ::= term5 '::' term4
                    | term5
  term5           ::= additive '=' additive
                    | additive compare additive
                    | additive
  compare         ::= 'is' | '=:=' | '=\=' | '<' | '=<' | '>' | '>='
  additive        ::= additive '+' multiplicative
                    | additive '-' multiplicative
                    | multiplicative
  multiplicative  ::= multiplicative mulop unary
                    | unary
  mulop           ::= '*' | '/' | '//' | 'div' | 'mod' | 'rem'
  unary           ::= '+' unary
                    | '-' unary
                    | application
  application     ::= application atom
                    | application large-id '\' term
                    | application small-id '\' term
                    | atom
  atom            ::= '(' term ')'
                    | '!' | 'true' | 'fail' | 'debug' | 's' | 'pi' | 'sigma'
                    | small-id | large-id | nat-lit | str-lit | chr-lit
                    | '[' ']'
                    | '[' elements ']'
  elements        ::= term5
                    | term5 ',' elements

The forms, from loosest to tightest binding, are:

  level     form                                 associativity
  0         X\ t                                 body extends to the right
  0         t :- t                               right
  1         t ; t                                left
  2         t => t                               right
  3         t , t                                left
  4         t :: t                               right
  5         t = t, t is t, t =:= t, t =\= t,     none
            t < t, t =< t, t > t, t >= t
  6         t + t, t - t                         left
  7         t * t, t / t, t // t, t div t,       left
            t mod t, t rem t
  8         + t, - t                             prefix
  9         t t, t X\ t                          left
  10        atoms, ( t ), [ ... ]

`X\ t` binds the identifier `X`, a `large-id` or a `small-id`, in `t`. The
body is a full `term` and extends as far to the right as the enclosing
parentheses, brackets, or terminating `.` allow; it swallows every binary
operator, including `,`, `=>`, `;`, and `:-`. Thus `pi X\ p X, q X` is
`pi (X\ (p X, q X))`, and `?- pi X\ true, Y = X.` fails because `Y = X` lies
inside the scope of `X` (Section 4.2); write `(pi X\ true), Y = X` to keep it
outside.

An abstraction appears without parentheses only as a whole `term` (the body
of a query or clause, the inside of parentheses, the body of another
abstraction, or the right operand of `:-`) or as the last argument of an
application: `f a X\ t` is `f a (X\ t)`. As the operand of any other operator
it MUST be parenthesized: `F = X\ X` is a parse error and must be written
`F = (X\ X)`, and `(X\ t) a` applies an abstraction. A reserved word such as
`s` cannot be a binder.

`:-` is right-associative and the loosest binary operator. Its left operand is
a `term1`, so a head that contains `:-` or an abstraction must be
parenthesized. `;` and `,` are left-associative; `=>` and `::` are
right-associative.

`=`, `is`, and the comparisons are non-associative, so `1 = 1 = 1` is a parse
error, and they bind tighter than `::`: `X = 1 :: []` parses as
`(X = 1) :: []`, which is ill-typed, and must be written `X = (1 :: [])`.

Application is left-associative. `pi`, `sigma`, `s`, `true`, `fail`, `!`, and
`debug` are atoms, so `pi X\ G` is the constant `pi` applied to the
abstraction `X\ G`, and `s 2` is an application.

`[]` is the empty list and `[t1, ..., tn]` abbreviates `t1 :: ... :: tn :: []`.
A list element is a `term5`, so an element that contains `::`, `,`, `=>`, `;`,
`:-`, or an abstraction MUST be parenthesized. A string literal `"abc"`
abbreviates `'a' :: 'b' :: 'c' :: []`. Parentheses only group.

2.4 Parse errors
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A syntax error is reported as one of

  *** parsing error at { row = R, col = C }.
  *** parsing error at EOF.

where (R, C) is the position of the token that the LALR(1) parser cannot
accept.


================================================================================
Chapter 3. Static Semantics
================================================================================

3.1 Processing order and the initial environment
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Loading a file processes its `kind` declarations in source order, then its
`type` declarations in source order, and then its clauses in source order.
Textual order between these classes is therefore not a visibility rule: a
`type` declaration may mention a type constructor whose `kind` declaration
comes later, and a clause may use a constant declared below it. All clauses
are type checked in source order before any of them is converted to clause
form (Sections 3.6 and 3.7); the first error in either pass aborts the load.

The initial kind environment is:

  o       : type
  nat     : type
  char    : type
  list    : type -> type
  string  : type            reserved; `string` abbreviates `list char`

The function type constructor `->` has kind `type -> type -> type` and is
written infix. None of these names can be redeclared. The initial constants
and their type schemes are:

  true, fail, !                         : o
  (,), (;), (=>), (:-)                  : o -> o -> o
  pi, sigma                             : (A -> o) -> o
  =                                     : A -> A -> o
  is, =:=, =\=, <, =<, >, >=            : nat -> nat -> o
  +, -, *, /, //, div, mod, rem         : nat -> nat -> nat
  unary +, unary -                      : nat -> nat
  s                                     : nat -> nat
  []                                    : list A
  ::                                    : A -> list A -> list A
  debug                                 : string -> o
  natural literal                       : nat
  character literal                     : char
  string literal                        : string

The initial program consists of the single clause `X = X.`, which defines
equality at every type (Section 4.3).

3.2 Kind declarations
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`kind c K.` declares the type constructor `c` with kind `K`. `K` MUST be
first-order: `type`, or `type -> K'` with `K'` first-order. A kind with an
arrow in argument position, such as `(type -> type) -> type`, is rejected. A
type constructor, including a built-in one, MUST NOT be declared twice.

3.3 Type declarations
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`type c T.` declares the constant `c` with the type scheme obtained by
generalizing `T` over all of its type variables. `T` MUST be well-kinded with
kind `type`: every type constructor in it must be declared (the kind
environment is complete by then), the argument of an application must have
the kind the constructor expects, and a type variable has kind `type`. `string`
in `T` is replaced by `list char` before kind checking.

The result type of `T`, the type remaining after stripping every top-level
`->`, MUST be headed by a declared type constructor other than `nat`, `char`,
and `list` (hence not `string`); a result headed by a type variable is
rejected as well. Consequently user constants construct user-declared types or
predicates over `o`, while `nat`, `char`, and lists are populated only by
literals, `s`, `[]`, and `::`. A constant MUST NOT be declared twice.

3.4 Variables, wildcards, and desugaring
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Within one declaration or one query, every free occurrence of the same
`large-id` other than `_` denotes the same variable; the scope of a free
variable is the whole declaration or query. Each occurrence of `_` denotes a
distinct anonymous variable. Anonymous variables of a query are never
displayed in an answer; a variable such as `_X` is an ordinary named variable
and is displayed.

`X\ t` binds `X` in `t`; an inner binder shadows an outer binder and free
occurrences of the same name. A `small-id` binder shadows the constant of
that name within its body: in `pi p\ p a`, `p` is the bound variable even
when a predicate `p` is declared, and outside the body `p` is the constant
again. (`_` as a binder name is accepted but, since every `_` in the body is
a fresh anonymous variable, it binds nothing.)

Desugaring also expands string literals into lists of characters and, after
type checking, beta-reduces the term completely: a redex such as `(X\ p X) a`
written in the source is reduced at compile time, so the clause `(X\ p X) a.`
is the clause `p a.`.

3.5 Type inference
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Types are inferred by unification. A constant is instantiated afresh from its
scheme at every occurrence; a natural literal has type `nat` and a character
literal type `char`; each variable has one monomorphic type throughout its
scope, and the occurrences of a free variable are unified with each other; an
application `t u` requires `t : A -> B` and `u : A` and has type `B`; an
abstraction `X\ t` has type `A -> B` when `X : A` and `t : B`. A clause and a
query MUST have type `o`.

Type variables that remain undetermined in a clause are generalized: the
clause is polymorphic, and its type parameters are instantiated afresh on
every use (Section 4.4). Type variables that remain undetermined in a query
become type-level logic variables, which unification may instantiate; types
are never displayed.

A failure is reported as

  *** typechecking-error[R1:C1-R2:C2]:
    ? expected_typ = `...', actual_typ = `...'.
    ? couldn't solve the equation `...' ~ `...',
    ? because ...

naming the expected and actual types of the offending subterm and the
equation that failed (a mismatch, a kind mismatch, or an occurs-check
failure). Undetermined type variables are shown as `A_0`, `A_1`, and so on.

3.6 Clause and goal forms
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

After type checking, every clause and every query MUST have one of the forms

  clause    ::= 'pi' X\ clause
              | atom ':-' goal
              | atom
  atom      ::= c t1 ... tn           c a declared constant, n >= 0
              | X t1 ... tn           only inside the antecedent of `=>`
  goal      ::= 'true' | 'fail' | '!' | 'debug' t
              | goal ',' goal
              | goal ';' goal
              | clause '=>' goal
              | 'pi' X\ goal
              | 'sigma' X\ goal
              | t '=' t
              | t 'is' t | t compare t
              | t                     any other term of type `o`

where heads are examined after beta-reduction. The rules are:

  * A clause head is an application of a declared constant. A logical
    operator cannot be a head (`true :- q.` and `(p, q) :- r.` are rejected
    as invalid clause heads), and `=` cannot be given a clause (`1 = 1.` is
    rejected).
  * A variable head is allowed only in a hypothesis, the antecedent of `=>`:
    `pi (P\ P => P)` is valid; a program clause `pi X\ X a.` is not.
  * A clause body is a goal. `:-` MUST NOT occur as a goal: `p :- (q :- r).`
    is rejected.
  * The antecedent of `=>` is a single clause. A logical operator or a
    conjunction of clauses as antecedent is rejected as an invalid clause
    head; write `D1 => D2 => G`.
  * Only the operands of control operators are examined as goals. The
    arguments of an atom are values: `store (true => true).` is a valid
    clause whose argument happens to be a term of type `o`.
  * A goal headed by a variable (for example the body of `call G :- G.`) and
    `pi F` or `sigma F` with a variable `F` are accepted statically; Section
    4.8 says what happens when such a goal is selected while still unbound.

Violations are reported as `*** converting-error[loc]:` followed by one of

    ? invalid clause head: expected an atomic predicate, optionally preceded
      by pi or followed by :-.
    ? the built-in predicate `=' cannot be given a clause.
    ? `:-' cannot occur as a goal.

3.7 Elaboration
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A clause is closed before it enters the program: its free term variables are
universally quantified in front of it, and its remaining type variables are
quantified outside those, so a clause with free variables `X1`, ..., `Xn`
behaves as `pi X1\ ... pi Xn\ clause`. The quantifiers are instantiated with
fresh logic variables every time the clause is used (Section 4.4). The
built-in clause `X = X.` is closed the same way.

A query is elaborated into a goal whose named free variables are logic
variables of level 0 and whose anonymous variables are logic variables that
the answer never shows.


================================================================================
Chapter 4. Dynamic Semantics
================================================================================

4.1 Terms and equality
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The runtime works with beta-normal lambda terms over constants, logic
variables, and bound variables. Two terms are equal when their beta-normal
forms coincide up to the names of bound variables; eta is not applied. The
user's variable and binder names are not part of a term, which is why printed
terms use generated binder names (Section 5.4).

Natural numbers are unary numerals: a literal `n+1` and the term `s n` are
the same term. Hence `3 = s 2` succeeds, `X = s Y, X = 3` binds `Y := 2`, and
the clause `add (s M) N (s P) :- add M N P.` resolves the goal `add 2 3 N`.
Arithmetic expressions are ordinary terms until `is` or a comparison
evaluates them (Section 4.7): `1 + 2 = 3` fails.

Polymorphic constants carry their type instances as hidden arguments. These
take part in unification like any other argument and are erased from every
display.

4.2 Levels
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The query's logic variables and every declared constant have level 0, and the
query is proved at level 0. Proving `pi X\ G` at level l creates a fresh
constant c of level l+1 and proves `G[c/X]` at level l+1. `sigma X\ G` at
level l creates a fresh logic variable of level l. The variables created when
a clause is used for a goal at level l have level l.

A logic variable of level l MUST only be bound to terms whose constants have
level at most l, except that a deeper constant MAY occur where it is one of
the variable's own arguments (Section 4.3). When a variable of level l is
bound, every logic variable occurring in the bound term gets level
min(its own level, l), so visibility restrictions propagate through bindings.
This is what makes

  ?- pi X\ Y = X.                 fail: Y has level 0, X has level 1
  ?- pi X\ sigma Y\ Y = X.        succeed: Y is created at level 1
  ?- pi X\ true, Y = X.           fail: Y = X is inside the scope of X

4.3 Unification
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`T = U` and clause selection use higher-order pattern unification over
beta-normal terms, extended with the level discipline of Section 4.2. A
*pattern* is a flexible term `F x1 ... xn` whose arguments are distinct
bound variables or distinct constants of a level deeper than `F`. The rules,
applied to the current set of disagreements until none is left, are:

  * rigid-rigid: two terms with the same head and the same number of
    arguments are decomposed into their argument pairs; otherwise the branch
    fails. Two numerals agree exactly when they are equal.
  * abstraction against abstraction: the bodies are unified. Abstraction
    against a rigid term: the rigid term is eta-expanded first.
  * pattern against a term: the variable is bound. The arguments of the
    pattern are abstracted over; a constant of a deeper level that is not an
    argument makes the branch fail; a logic variable occurring in the term is
    pruned to what both sides may see; and an occurrence of the variable
    itself in the term (occurs check) makes the branch fail.
  * pattern against pattern, with the same variable or with different
    variables: solved with a fresh variable applied to the common arguments.
  * A flexible subterm of the bound term that is not a pattern is kept as it
    is, with its arguments expressed through the pattern's arguments, so that
    `?- X = g (F a).` binds `X := g (F a)` and `?- F = (x\ G x x).` binds
    `F := W_1\ G W_1 W_1`. When its arguments cannot be expressed that way,
    for instance because they mention a constant the bound variable cannot
    see, or the bound variable itself, the whole disagreement is deferred
    instead.
  * A disagreement whose flexible side is not a pattern is deferred. It is
    kept as a *residual disagreement* of the branch, is re-examined after
    every later binding in the branch, and is reported with the answer when
    it is still open at the end. Deferral alone never fails a branch.

For example `?- F a = b.` and `?- F X = a.` succeed with the residual
disagreements `F a =?= b` and `F X =?= a` respectively, because `a` is a
level-0 constant visible to `F` and `X` is a logic variable, so neither
application is a pattern.

4.4 Proof search
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The runtime state is a stack of alternatives. An alternative is a branch:
the bindings made so far, the levels of its variables and constants, its
residual disagreements, and a list of pending goals, each carrying the
hypotheses installed for it and the level at which it is proved. Search is
depth-first and left-to-right: the first pending goal of the top alternative
is selected, and failure pops the top alternative and continues with the next
one. When an alternative runs out of goals, it is an answer (Section 4.9);
when the stack is empty, the query has no further answers.

A goal headed by a logical operator is handled as in Section 4.5. A goal
`p t1 ... tn` headed by any other constant is resolved against the clauses
about `p`, in this order: the hypotheses of the goal, most recently installed
first, and then the clauses of the program in source order, the built-in
`X = X.` being the first of them. A hypothesis whose head is a variable is
considered for every goal but matches only when that variable is bound to an
application of `p`. Resolution of a clause instantiates its quantifiers with
fresh variables of the goal's level, unifies its head with the goal together
with the branch's residual disagreements, and, on success, pushes an
alternative whose pending goals are the clause body followed by the remaining
goals. The alternatives for one goal are pushed so that the first clause is
explored first. A resolution is identified by a fresh *call id*, which the
body goals carry for the purposes of cut.

4.5 Goal forms
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  true            succeed
  fail            fail the branch
  G1 , G2         prove G1, then G2 in the resulting state
  G1 ; G2         prove G1; on backtracking, prove G2 instead
  D => G          install the clause D as a hypothesis and prove G; D is tried
                  before every other clause while G is being proved
  sigma X\ G      prove G with a fresh logic variable in place of X
  pi X\ G         prove G with a fresh constant one level deeper in place of X
  !               commit as in Section 4.6, then succeed
  T = U           unify T with U (Section 4.3)
  L is R          evaluate R and unify L with the result (Section 4.7)
  comparisons     evaluate both sides and compare them (Section 4.7)
  debug S         print `*** debugger called with S` and switch step debugging
                  on for the rest of the query (Section 5.3)
  p t1 ... tn     resolve against the clauses about p (Section 4.4)

The scope of a hypothesis is the proof of `G`: it is visible to every goal
proved while proving `G`, including the bodies of clauses resolved for them,
and it disappears once `G` has been proved. Hypotheses installed by an inner
`=>` come before those of an outer one, and all hypotheses come before the
program.

4.6 Cut
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`!` discards every alternative that was created at or after the selection of
the clause whose body contains the cut: the remaining clauses for that goal,
the choice points created by goals to the left of the cut in the same body
(including those of `;` and of nested calls), and the corresponding
alternatives of hypotheses. Alternatives created before that selection, by
the callers, survive. A cut is transparent to `,`, `;`, `=>`, `pi`, and
`sigma` within the body. In a query, a cut discards every alternative of the
query, including the right branch of an enclosing `;`:

  ?- p X, !, fail ; true.         fails

With `t X :- r X, !.`, `t 3.`, `r 1.`, and `r 2.`, the query `?- t X.`
answers `X := 1` and nothing else.

4.7 Arithmetic
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`L is R`, `=:=`, `=\=`, `<`, `=<`, `>`, and `>=` evaluate their operands
immediately, when the goal is selected. Evaluation maps a term to a natural
number: a literal to its value, `s t` to the value of `t` plus one, `+ t` to
the value of `t`, `- t` to its negation (an error unless the value is zero),
and `t1 op t2` to the operation on the values, where `/` is exact division,
`//` and `div` are integer division, and `mod` and `rem` are the remainder
(on naturals the members of each pair coincide). Evaluation is an error, not
a failure, in these cases:

  instantiation_error                     an operand is an unbound variable
  type_error(evaluable, T)                T is not an arithmetic term
  domain_error(not_less_than_zero, N)     a subtraction or negation yields N < 0
  domain_error(nat, A / B)                A is not a multiple of B
  evaluation_error(zero_divisor)          division or remainder by zero

`L is R` evaluates `R` and unifies `L` with the resulting numeral, so
`X is 1 + 2` binds `X := 3`, `3 is 1 + 2` succeeds, and `1 + 2 is 3` fails
because `1 + 2` is not a numeral. A comparison evaluates both sides and
succeeds or fails. Arithmetic is never delayed: `?- X is Y + 1.` is an
instantiation error even though a later goal might have bound `Y`.

4.8 Runtime errors
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A runtime error abandons the whole query: every alternative is discarded, no
answer is printed, and the REPL prints one of

  *** runtime-error: bad goal given!
  *** runtime-error: bad fact given!
  *** runtime-error: <arithmetic error>

"bad goal given" arises when the selected goal is not headed by a constant,
typically a variable goal that is still unbound (`?- call X.` with
`call G :- G.`). "bad fact given" arises when a hypothesis selected for
resolution is not a clause, for example a variable hypothesis that has been
bound to `true` or to a conjunction. The arithmetic errors are those of
Section 4.7.

4.9 Answers
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When an alternative has no pending goals, it is an answer. The answer is
projected onto the query's named variables: for each named variable `X` that
is bound, the line `X := t.` is printed with `t` fully substituted, except
that a binding of `X` to itself or to an internal variable that merely stands
for `X` is dropped; such an internal variable is renamed to `X` wherever it
occurs in the displayed answer, so `?- X = Y.` answers `Y := X.`. Anonymous
and internal variables are never listed. The residual disagreements of the
branch are printed after the bindings. The REPL shows

  The answer substitution is:
  X := t.
  ...
  The remaining disagreements are:
  T =?= U.
  ...
  Find more solutions? [Y/n]

omitting each block that is empty. An answer of `y`, `yes`, or `yes.` in any
letter case continues the search with the next alternative; an empty answer
repeats the question; any other answer stops the search, and the REPL prints
`yes.`. When nothing would be displayed at all, that is, when the query has no
named variables or all of their bindings are trivial and no residual
disagreement remains, the REPL prints `yes.` at once without offering further
solutions. When the search runs out of alternatives, the REPL prints `no.`.


================================================================================
Chapter 5. REPL and Module Loading
================================================================================

5.1 Starting and loading
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

`ppap` dispatches to ALPHA1 when `ALPHA1` is entered at its prompt. ALPHA1
then prompts `hol =<< ` for a module path. An empty line answers
`hol> Ok, no module loaded.` and starts the REPL with the initial program
only, under the prompt `hol>`. Otherwise the path MUST have no extension or
the extension `.hol`; `.hol` is appended when missing.
Another extension is reported with `*** loading-error: ... is not the
extension of a hol module; expected `.hol'.`, an unreadable file with
`*** loading-error: couldn't read the file ...`, and the path is asked for
again. Loading prints

  hol> Compiling <module> ( <absolute path>, interpreted )

and then either an error (Chapters 1 through 3) followed by a new prompt for a
path, or `<module>> Ok, one module loaded.` followed by the REPL under the
prompt `<module>>`. The module name is the path of the file relative to the
working directory, without extension and with `.` for the directory
separator (`example.einstein`); a file outside the working directory is named
by its base name. Only one module is loaded at a time, and the program is the
initial program plus the file's declarations.

5.2 The query prompt
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

At the prompt, an empty line is ignored and the following inputs are special:

  :q          quit ALPHA1
  :d          toggle step debugging for the following queries (the REPL
              answers `Debugging mode on.` or `Debugging mode off.`)

Any other line MUST be a query `?- G.`; a declaration is rejected with
`*** parsing-error: it is not a query.`, and lexical, syntactic, type, and
clause-form errors are printed as in the loader. A query is elaborated and
proved as in Chapter 4, and the session continues after `yes.`, `no.`, or a
runtime error. End of input quits.

5.3 Step debugging
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Step debugging is on for a query when the REPL flag was on at its start or
when the query executes `debug S`. While it is on, the runtime prints, before
each step, the current alternative and the rest of the stack: the pending
goals with their call ids, the levels of the visible constants and variables,
the bindings of the query variables, the residual disagreements, and the
branch's thread id. It then asks

  Press the enter key to go to next state:

An empty line performs the step. `:d` turns stepping off for the rest of the
query (`Debugging mode off for the rest of this query.`), and `:q` quits
ALPHA1. Neither `debug` nor `:d` at this prompt changes the REPL flag.

5.4 Printed terms
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Terms are printed in the grammar of Chapter 2 with the precedences of Section
2.3, parenthesized where needed, after beta-normalization. Numerals are
printed in decimal, lists as `[t1, ..., tn]`, lists of characters as string
literals, and characters as character literals. Type arguments are erased.
Bound variables are printed as `W_1`, `W_2`, ..., counting from the outside
and skipping names that clash with the logic variables of the term; an
abstraction that is the last argument of an application is printed without
parentheses, as in `pi W_1\ p W_1`. Named logic variables are printed by
their names, anonymous and internally generated logic variables as `?V_n`,
constants introduced by `pi` as `?c_n`, and type variables as `?TV_n`; none
of these spellings is an identifier of the source language.


================================================================================
Chapter 6. Differences from Hol BETA (Informative)
================================================================================

ALPHA1 is not a subset of BETA in every respect. A BETA reader should note:

  * No modules, module headers, or imports; one file is the whole program.
  * No fixity, abbreviation, or notation declarations; no `&`; no multi-head
    clauses; no `print`, `read`, or `presburger`; no `:reload`, snapshots,
    `:show`, `:assign`, `:short`, or `:verbose`.
  * Hypotheses installed by `=>` are tried before the program's clauses
    (BETA tries global clauses first).
  * `=` has only the built-in clause: equalities and comparisons cannot be
    hypotheses, and `=` cannot be given a clause.
  * Arithmetic is immediate: `is` and the comparisons evaluate when selected,
    and an evaluation error aborts the query instead of becoming a residual
    constraint or a failure.
  * There are no quoted or symbolic identifiers, and `s`, `pi`, `sigma`,
    `is`, `div`, `mod`, and `rem` are reserved words, so none of them can be
    a lambda binder.
  * An abstraction is unparenthesized only as a whole term or as the last
    argument of an application, and its body extends as far right as the
    enclosing delimiters allow.
  * A conjunction cannot be the antecedent of `=>`.
  * Residual disagreements are printed as they are, under
    `The remaining disagreements are:`; there is no existential projection.
  * A runtime error discards every alternative of the query.
